Campervan Gas and Carbon Monoxide Safety: Alarms, Ventilation and an Exit Plan

A practical campervan gas safety guide covering CO alarms, ventilation, system checks and what to do when an alarm sounds.

A campervan hob has a small flame, but poor ventilation or a faulty appliance can create a very large problem. Carbon monoxide, or CO, is particularly dangerous because it has no colour or smell. Your nose cannot replace an alarm. LPG may have a noticeable odour when it leaks, but smell should never be the whole safety system either.

The reassuring part is wonderfully unglamorous: safe van travel depends on suitable appliances, clear ventilation, working alarms and a simple exit plan. This guide puts those pieces into an order you can use before a trip and at every overnight stop.

CO, an LPG leak and a fire are different hazards

HazardWhat may reveal itCore protection
Carbon monoxideNo colour or smell; possible headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea and confusionAudible CO alarm, maintained appliances and ventilation
LPG leakAn added odour may be noticeable, but smell alone is not a safety systemSound installation, shutoff valves, hose checks and a suitable gas alarm
Smoke and fireSmoke, heat, flame or an alarmSmoke alarm, extinguisher, fire blanket and a clear exit

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion among common CO poisoning symptoms. If several people in the van develop flu like symptoms together, get everyone into fresh air. Finding the culprit can wait.

Which alarms belong in a campervan?

Start with an audible carbon monoxide alarm and a smoke alarm designed for the conditions inside a recreational vehicle. A campervan with LPG should also have an appropriate gas alarm where the system and local guidance call for one. These devices detect different hazards, so read the specification before trusting an all in one label.

  • choose a model approved by its manufacturer for a motorhome, caravan or boat
  • fit it at the position and height stated in its manual
  • do not hide it behind curtains, cupboards or hanging coats
  • test it at the intervals set by the maker and record its replacement date
  • run the sound test again after changing a battery

The US National Park Service RV safety guide recommends working carbon monoxide and propane detectors and knowing every door, hatch and emergency exit. An alarm still sealed in its box is just an expensive key ring.

What should never be used indoors?

Keep portable camping stoves, charcoal barbecues, fire pits and generators outside. Never carry glowing coals into the van for a moment, and never use cooker burners to heat the cabin. Equipment built for outdoor use does not become safe merely because it is raining.

Official nidirect guidance warns against bringing portable cookers, lights, heaters or a lit or smouldering barbecue into a caravan. Indoors, use only permanently installed appliances intended for that job and maintained correctly.

Ventilation is part of the safety system

Fixed vents are not failed attempts at climate control. They provide combustion air and help products of combustion leave safely. Never tape them over when the temperature drops. Check that leaves, mud, luggage or snow have not blocked an external flue or heater outlet.

The UK Health and Safety Executive says ventilation must not be blocked, flues must remain clear and appliances should be serviced. In practical van terms, follow the appliance maintenance schedule and inspect vents and outlets before using the heater after driving or storage.

A ten minute pre trip check

  1. Open the cylinder locker. Check the restraint, visible hose and regulator. Cracks, corrosion or damage mean no gas use until a competent professional has checked the system.
  2. Inspect the outlets. Remove leaves and other obstructions from vents and the heater flue.
  3. Test every alarm. Each one should sound clearly, and sleepers in every part of the van must be able to hear it.
  4. Locate the shutoff valves. Every adult should know how to isolate the gas safely.
  5. Clear the escape route. Shoes, folding chairs and storage boxes must not block a door or emergency window.
  6. Check the extinguisher. Keep it accessible near an exit, not beneath the holiday luggage.

You can store inspection dates, alarm replacement dates and insurance reminders in the Travel Rules app. A timely notification is much cheaper than hunting for a workshop on a Sunday evening.

The two minute bedtime routine

  • never leave cooking unattended and turn burners off after use
  • do not dry clothes on or immediately beside a heater
  • keep permanent vents open as the conversion manual requires
  • move paper, cloth and other combustibles away from the hob
  • keep keys and a torch reachable from the bed
  • agree which exit everyone will use in an emergency

What to do when an alarm sounds

  1. Wake everyone and move into fresh air. Do not stop to pack.
  2. If it can be done immediately without risk, switch the appliance off and isolate the gas. Never re enter a smoky cabin to do it.
  3. Call the emergency services if anyone has symptoms, there is a fire or you cannot identify the cause.
  4. Do not return or restart appliances until the cause has been safely identified and corrected.

Headache, dizziness, vomiting, confusion, chest pain or loss of consciousness require urgent medical attention. Feeling better after a few minutes outside does not prove the exposure was harmless.

Three easy ways to get it wrong

Cooking while it rains

Closing every window feels cosy, but cooking consumes oxygen and creates moisture. Use the appliance exactly as directed, provide the required ventilation and never use the hob as a heater.

Running the heater overnight

Only an appliance designed, installed and maintained for overnight operation should be used that way. The CO alarm must work, permanent ventilation must remain clear and the external flue must be unobstructed.

A generator or engine beside a window

Exhaust can enter through an opening. The CDC advises operating a generator outdoors and more than 20 feet, about 6 metres, from doors, windows and vents. Watch neighbouring vehicles too, especially on crowded stopovers.

Save this campervan gas safety checklist

  • CO, smoke and LPG alarms: suitable, installed and tested
  • permanent vents and flue outlet: clear
  • cylinder, regulator and visible hoses: no visible damage
  • extinguisher and fire blanket: accessible
  • gas shutoff and emergency exit: known to the whole crew
  • portable stove, barbecue and generator: outdoors only
  • next service date: recorded before the next journey

A safe campervan does not need to look like a laboratory. It needs to warn you in time, breathe as designed and let everyone get out quickly. Frequently asked questions

Will a smoke alarm detect carbon monoxide?

Do not assume it will. Smoke and CO are different hazards. Check the exact specification and use separate suitable alarms unless the manufacturer explicitly confirms a combined function.

Where should a CO alarm be fitted in a campervan?

There is no universal position for every model. Follow the alarm and conversion manuals, paying attention to sleeping areas, combustion appliances and the clearances stated by the manufacturer.

Can you sleep with a gas heater running?

Only if the appliance and conversion manufacturers allow that operating mode, the installation is maintained, ventilation is clear and a working CO alarm is fitted. A portable camping heater is not a substitute for a campervan heating system.
