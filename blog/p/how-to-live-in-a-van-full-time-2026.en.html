How to Live in a Van Full Time Without Hating It: The Brutally Honest 2026 Guide

What is full time van life really like? A practical guide to systems, costs, routines, mistakes and your first 30 days, with none of the Instagram varnish.

In photographs, van life is coffee by an open door with the ocean parked neatly at the foot of the bed. On an ordinary Tuesday, it is more likely to involve finding a tap, drying a towel and moving the same bag for the seventh time because the thing you need is underneath it.

That is not an argument against living in a van. It is an argument for beginning with your eyes open. Full time van life can deliver remarkable freedom, but it does not remove daily chores. It swaps rent, a bathroom and a wardrobe for fuel, logistics and a surprisingly intimate relationship with the level of your fresh water tank.

This guide explains how to live in a van full time without romanticising it or turning every inconvenience into a warning siren. You get practical systems, an honest budget method, repeatable routines and a plan for your first 30 days.

Rent first, sell your furniture later

The most expensive beginner mistake has nothing to do with solar panels. It is buying a vehicle before learning whether you enjoy daily life in a small space. A weekend is not enough. A weekend forgives wet shoes, no shower and a bed that folds like angry origami. After ten days, tiny inconveniences begin holding a referendum on your patience.

Before investing, run three tests: four wet days, one normal working week and ten days that include regular driving. Cook, work, do laundry and find overnight parking exactly as you would while living in the van. The useful question is not “was it beautiful?” It is “does this rhythm still work when the weather and mobile signal refuse to cooperate?”

The six systems that decide whether van life feels easy

SleepFlat bed, ventilation and proper blackoutYou wake up sore every morning
WaterSeparate drinking and utility suppliesYou cannot estimate how many days remain
PowerA watt hour budget for every deviceEvery evening becomes a charging lottery
ToiletA daily plan and an emergency optionEvery stop begins with a toilet search
VentilationIntake, exhaust and airflow under the mattressWater runs down the windows each morning
StorageOne permanent home for every objectFinding a cable requires unpacking the van

Do not buy electrical equipment from a random conversion video. Calculate your own use first. A laptop drawing 60 W for five hours needs roughly 300 Wh. Add the phone, router, fridge, lights and inverter losses. Only that total tells you what battery and charging system make sense.

Do the same with water. Spend a week measuring how much you use for drinking, cooking and washing. Without that measurement, a 70 litre tank is just a large number occupying a large cupboard.

A van life budget without a magical internet average

There is no honest single answer to “how much does van life cost?” Someone staying for a week in one place has a completely different budget from someone driving 300 kilometres every day. Instead of copying a monthly total from a stranger, create four envelopes.

  1. Fixed: insurance, tax, finance, phone, data and subscriptions.
  2. Daily life: food, laundry, showers, parking and campsites.
  3. Movement: fuel, tolls, ferries and city parking.
  4. Failure: servicing, tyres, leisure batteries, heating and the parts that always fail while it is raining.

Calculate fuel with a simple formula: planned kilometres multiplied by consumption per 100 km, divided by 100, then multiplied by the price per litre. Add a repair reserve even when the van has just passed inspection. Mechanical parts do not read your calendar.

Where to sleep without waking to blue lights

Europe has no single shared law for sleeping in a van. Parking rules are set by countries, regions and municipalities. Legal parking does not always mean camping is allowed. Chairs, an awning, levelling blocks, a barbecue or draining water can turn a parked vehicle into a campsite under local rules.

Before sleeping, read the signs at the entrance, check recent reports and look at the municipal website. Our detailed guide to where to sleep in a van for free in Europe explains the country by country basics. Always save a second location offline. Plan B is much more charming at 18:00 than at 01:20.

A boring morning routine beats an expensive organiser

Fifteen minutes at night

  • confirm that the spot is legal and identify the exit
  • secure every loose object before bed
  • check water, battery level and the morning weather
  • cover windows without blocking ventilation
  • put shoes, torch and keys in one known place

Fifteen minutes in the morning

  • air the van and lift the mattress
  • dispose of rubbish at a proper collection point
  • record water and energy use during the first month
  • check the route, fuel and vehicle height
  • leave the place cleaner than you found it

This routine sounds dull. Excellent. A dull system frees your attention for the journey instead of making you hunt for keys inside the bedding.

Remote work needs two internet connections and a real desk

If your income depends on connectivity, do not rely on one phone and one SIM. Carry two connections from different networks, keep a map of libraries or coworking spaces and have a separate plan for important calls. Download files in advance. A speed test at night does not guarantee a stable meeting in the morning when the car park fills up.

Raise the screen and support your back. Working eight hours with a laptop on your knees is an excellent way to meet physiotherapists in several countries. Our guide to remote work, internet and power in a van goes deeper.

Hygiene, laundry and the toilet question

Build a network of options instead of searching for one perfect bathroom. Campsites, swimming pools, gyms, marinas, laundromats and public facilities solve different pieces of the problem. Keep a small emergency wash kit and a drinking water reserve that never gets sacrificed to dirty dishes.

A chemical or separating toilet must be emptied at an approved point. Grey water does not belong on a car park or in a storm drain. If your sanitation plan only works when the sun is shining and an empty campsite happens to be nearby, it is not a system. It is a wish.

Safety: an exit plan matters more than clever gadgets

  • park facing the easiest exit
  • keep electronics out of view
  • keep keys within reach but away from the doors
  • do not publish your exact location in real time
  • fit working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms
  • keep an extinguisher near the exit, not behind the bed
  • if a place feels wrong, leave without holding a committee meeting with your intuition

Solo and couple van life are different projects

Solo travellers control the pace but own every task. Couples can divide driving, cooking and logistics, but need explicit privacy rules. Decide who makes the final call on leaving a location, how much time each person needs alone and which shelf belongs to whom. A “shared drawer” quickly becomes an archaeological site.

Your first 30 days

  1. Days 1 to 3: stay close to home and test sleep, water and packing.
  2. Days 4 to 7: work normal days and complete one laundry cycle.
  3. Week 2: drive less, measure actual costs and track consumption.
  4. Week 3: test cold, rain or heat in safe conditions.
  5. Week 4: remove unused objects and upgrade only systems that genuinely failed.

After a month, ask three questions. Are you sleeping well? Is the budget stable? Does the daily logistics still leave room for a life? If two answers are no, do not buy a larger battery for emotional support. Change the rhythm or the vehicle.

Who should not begin with full time van life?

If you need complete privacy every day, struggle badly with changing plans or have medical care that requires constant access to one facility, begin with shorter journeys. Van life is not a courage exam. You can love travel and remain extremely fond of your own bathroom.

Checklist before moving into a van

  • a test lasting at least ten days
  • a three month budget and separate repair reserve
  • verified vehicle weight and documents
  • a power budget and measured water use
  • heating or cooling appropriate for the season
  • two internet options if you work remotely
  • a toilet, shower, laundry and waste plan
  • insurance that covers how the vehicle is actually used
  • offline overnight options and emergency contacts

Frequently asked questions

Can you legally live in a van in Europe?

You can travel and sleep in a van, but parking and camping rules are local. There is no single permission that covers all of Europe.

How much water does one person need?

It depends on cooking, washing and access to showers. Measure your own use for a week, add an emergency reserve and size the tank from real data.

Is van life cheaper than renting?

It can be if you limit mileage, avoid expensive vehicle finance and keep a repair fund. Frequent driving, ferries, tolls and failures can erase the saving quickly.

What is the best van to live in?

One in which you sleep well, remain within the legal weight and can obtain parts along your route. The badge on the bonnet cannot rescue a bad interior layout.
